## Attribution, composition and preservation

**Ascension of Isaiah.** Its Isaiah attribution is a literary voice, not verified authorship by the historical prophet. Jeremiah Coogan's 2021 scholarly introduction describes a Roman-Mediterranean work often dated to the second century CE, with martyrdom (1–5) and visionary (6–11) units and competing composition hypotheses. The fullest form survives in Geʿez, translated in late antiquity, while Greek, Latin, Slavonic and Coptic witnesses preserve differing forms. Geʿez preservation therefore does not license calling every reconstructed form an Ethiopian canonical book. [RP2] (C-scholarship). Older source-division hypotheses are quoted on the ECW introduction (Knibb and Müller); their precise dates and interpolation assignments should remain attributed theories, not metadata certainties. [RP3] (C-scholarship-as-quoted). The guide's anonymity must be preserved; the text distinguishes him from the angel of the Holy Spirit. [RP1, 7:2–8; 9:36] (C-text).

**4 Baruch / Paralipomena of Jeremiah / Rest of the Words of Baruch.** These are work-title aliases, not permission to merge it with 2 Baruch, 3 Baruch or every Ethiopian Jeremiah/Baruch work. The longer translation used here is Kraft and Ann Elizabeth Purintun's 1971/1972 text in a CATSS electronic edition. The shorter translation is William Newby's; it is not the selected basis. [RP4] (C-bibliography). SBL's description of Jens Herzer's 2005 critical edition presents his argument for an early-second-century Jewish work, preserved by Christians who added its ending. This is an attributed scholarly model; the full commentary was not accessed. [RP5] (C-publisher-account). The *Encyclopaedia Judaica* entry records transmission in Greek, Slavonic, Ethiopic, Coptic and Armenian and warns about unresolved textual relationships. [RP6] (C-scholarship). Harris's 1889 title proposes 136 CE; its publication catalogue verifies that historical proposal, not its correctness. [RP7] (C-bibliography). Do not convert the story's 66-year sleep into an independently certain composition date or exile chronology.

**History boundary.** The second work narrates Jeremiah accompanying captives to Babylon, a miraculous sleep, and a foundation of Samaria. These are this work's accounts; no historical reconstruction of Jeremiah's itinerary or Samaritan origins is established here. Its exclusion of mixed families and hostile portrayals belong to the narrator/characters, never to the atlas's own evaluation of peoples. Likewise Isaiah's martyrdom, the Beloved's disguised descent and visionary geography remain work-specific narrative. Cross-links to biblical figures are thematic identities within these retellings, not independent corroboration. [RP1; RP14–RP19] (C-text).
